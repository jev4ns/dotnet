The Lead

Boston's Housing First Programs Are on the Chopping Block — and Thousands of Lives Hang in the Balance

Picture this: a 14-person home in Northampton where every resident has a complex medical condition and spent years living on the street before finding stable housing. It works. The science backs it up. And now the Trump administration wants to gut the federal funding that makes it possible. A looming policy shift from the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development is threatening to unravel the "Housing First" model that has quietly kept nearly 4,000 Massachusetts households off the streets — to the tune of roughly $91 million in federal support.

"Housing First" is exactly what it sounds like: get people housed first, then address mental health and substance use. Two decades of evidence supports the approach. But the Trump administration wants to condition housing assistance on sobriety and treatment compliance — a reversal that advocates say will simply push the most vulnerable people back onto the streets. Programs across the state are already declining to accept new clients in anticipation of the cuts. One resident of the Northampton facility put it plainly: "You want to put me back on the street?"

What happens next depends largely on whether Massachusetts can backfill the federal dollars — and there's no sign Beacon Hill has a plan to do that. With Governor Healey already squeezing the state budget to find $100 million in health insurance savings (more on that below), the math is brutal. Advocates are pushing hard for legislative action before the cuts take effect, but the clock is ticking.

What Else You Should Know

White Stadium's Price Tag Tripled — and City Hall Sat on the News for Weeks. Newly released public records show Boston had a building permit in hand on January 8th that pegged the White Stadium rebuild at $134 million — nearly triple the original $50 million estimate — but Mayor Michelle Wu didn't tell the public until a February 6th press conference. That's at least four weeks of silence on a nearly $85 million gap in taxpayer costs for a professional soccer stadium. The City Council voted last week to block a transparency request from Councilor Ed Flynn. Not a great look.

State Employees Are Losing GLP-1 Coverage — 22,000 People Affected. The Massachusetts Group Insurance Commission voted 10-7 last week to drop coverage of weight-loss drugs like Ozempic and Wegovy for the more than 460,000 people on the state's employee health plan. The $46 million annual cost was simply too much for a tight budget, and Governor Healey pushed for the cut. Opponents warn it could worsen chronic conditions and deepen health disparities — meaning the "savings" might be an illusion.

Boston.com →

Healey and Campbell to Massachusetts Students: Know Your Rights. After federal agents reportedly posed as police searching for a missing child to gain entry to a Columbia University residence hall and arrest a student without a warrant, Governor Maura Healey and Attorney General Andrea Campbell issued statewide guidance: ICE needs a judicial warrant to enter dorms and other non-public campus spaces, and you have the right to demand one. The message was pointed — Healey called the Columbia incident "outrageous" — and it's clearly aimed at calming anxiety on Massachusetts campuses before something similar happens here.

ICE and Boston Are Fighting Over the Numbers on Detainer Requests. Acting ICE Director Todd Lyons went public Monday claiming Boston Police ignored 167 immigration detainer requests in 2025 — more than twice the 57 figure cited by Police Commissioner Michael Cox. Lyons says every one of them involved criminal charges. The Boston City Council voted 9-4 last week to block a request requiring Mayor Wu to detail which detainer requests BPD declined. The gap between the city's and the federal government's accounting is significant, and it's not getting resolved quietly.

Pressley Endorses Markey, Closing the Door on Her Own Senate Ambitions. U.S. Representative Ayanna Pressley, who spent months being talked about as a potential challenger to Senator Ed Markey, is now formally in his corner — backing him over fellow Representative Seth Moulton in what's shaping up to be a real primary fight. Pressley's decision consolidates the progressive lane around the 79-year-old incumbent and removes what would have been the most formidable potential challenger. Moulton, meanwhile, is leaning hard on the generational change argument that got Pressley to Congress in the first place.

Abington Is Staring Down a Brutal Budget Choice. The Abington Select Board voted unanimously last week to put a series of override questions on the May ballot, asking residents to approve $3.6 million in new tax revenue to avoid cuts that include 30 school positions, the elimination of police in schools, and possibly losing a second ambulance. Town Manager Michael Maresco's message was stark: a "level-funded" budget isn't actually level — it's a real-dollar cut when costs keep rising. South Shore towns are increasingly caught in this squeeze, and Abington's override vote will be an early test of how much residents are willing to pay.

One More Thing

Governor Healey's "Gateway to Pre-K" initiative promised that by the end of 2026, every family of a 4-year-old in Massachusetts's 26 Gateway Cities would have access to free or low-cost preschool — a genuinely ambitious goal. CommonWealth Beacon reports that seven cities still haven't joined the program, and providers in cities that have say implementation is harder than the press releases suggest. Universal pre-K is a great bumper sticker; universal pre-K that actually works is a much heavier lift.
