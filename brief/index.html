Beacon Hill Doesn't Want You Reading Its Mail — And Now It's Asking the Courts to Help

In a move that will surprise no one who has watched Massachusetts lawmakers operate, the state Senate voted Thursday to ask the Supreme Judicial Court whether two high-profile ballot questions — one that would force the Legislature to comply with the public records law, and one that would overhaul the often-lucrative stipend system — are unconstitutional. The legal maneuver is rare (it hasn't been used in a decade), and importantly, any opinion the SJC issues would be advisory, not binding. But that's almost beside the point. The message to transparency advocates is clear: Beacon Hill is not going quietly.

The context here matters. Voters overwhelmingly approved a ballot question in 2024 to allow Auditor Diana DiZoglio to audit the Legislature — and the Legislature has spent the time since then finding every available reason not to cooperate. Now, with two more reform measures gaining momentum toward November, lawmakers are pre-emptively seeding doubt about their constitutionality. Senate President Cynthia Creem and her colleagues argue the measures could have "unintended consequences" and raise separation-of-powers concerns. Critics will note that the people most worried about those consequences are the ones who currently get to operate with near-total opacity.

What happens next: The SJC will weigh in, and while its opinion won't kill the ballot questions outright, it could complicate the campaigns for both measures and give undecided voters a reason to pause. Watch for the stipends question in particular — a "yes" vote could mean a significant pay cut for a lot of legislators, which tends to focus the mind wonderfully.

AG Campbell Sues Over Trump's Student Race Data Demand

Attorney General Andrea Joy Campbell is leading a multi-state lawsuit against the Trump administration's demand that colleges submit student data broken down by race and sex — with a March 18 deadline attached. The administration says it needs the data to enforce last year's Supreme Court ruling against affirmative action; Campbell and her co-plaintiffs say the rushed timeline sets schools up for accidental errors that could trigger fines, loss of funding, and federal investigations. With multiple Massachusetts universities in the mix, this one has real stakes for higher ed across the state.

World Cup Is (Finally) Coming to Foxborough

After months of standoff over who would guarantee $7.8 million in security costs, the Foxborough Select Board unanimously approved the funding deal Thursday — in a meeting that lasted less than 60 seconds. The Kraft Group has already wired over $1.5 million to the town, with future payments arriving before Foxborough has to write any checks. A formal vote on the full event license is set for Tuesday and is expected to be a formality. Seven World Cup matches at Gillette Stadium this summer are now essentially a go.

The Rent Control Battle Gets a New Weapon

Opponents of the rent control ballot question — which would cap annual increases at 5 percent statewide — dropped a big number Thursday: $300 billion. That's the projected loss in residential property values over a decade, according to a report from the Greater Boston Real Estate Board and the Center for State Policy Analysis at Tufts University. The ripple effect, the study argues, would gut municipal property tax revenue and force cities and towns to cut services or raise rates. Supporters of rent control say the study overstates the case and ignores the very real harm that unchecked rent increases are doing to residents right now. Either way, this fight is just warming up.

Massachusetts' Climate Commitments Are Getting Wobbly

A sharp piece of analysis from CommonWealth Beacon tracks how Massachusetts politicians — including Governor Maura Healey — have quietly shifted from climate champions to energy-cost-first pragmatists as household electricity and gas bills have soared. House Democrats floated weakening the state's 2030 emissions mandate, then pivoted to slashing $1 billion from the Mass Save efficiency program. Healey has largely sidestepped both proposals while staying laser-focused on "affordability." The piece raises a fair question: when the politics get hard, how deep do Massachusetts' climate commitments actually run?

Brookline Boosts Synagogue Patrols After Michigan Attack

Brookline police announced increased patrols around houses of worship Thursday after a man drove a vehicle into a Detroit-area synagogue in what investigators called a deliberate attack on the Jewish community. Brookline officials said there is no specific local threat, but given the town's large Jewish population, the precautionary response is exactly what you'd expect.

Boston Firefighter Recovering After Kuwait Drone Strike

Governor Healey confirmed this week that Army National Guard Major Mark Garcia — a seven-year Boston Fire Department veteran assigned to a Charlestown engine company — was wounded in the Iranian drone strike earlier this month that killed six U.S. service members. Garcia, 40, is recovering in Germany, and Healey said she expects to celebrate his return home in a couple of months.
