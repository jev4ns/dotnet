The Lead
The Blizzard Is Over. The Reckoning Is Just Beginning.

Governor Maura Healey lifted the state of emergency Friday night, officially closing the books on the Blizzard of '26 — but don't let that fool you into thinking the hard part is done. The nor'easter that walloped Southeastern Massachusetts earlier this week was historic by any measure: more than 291,000 customers lost power at the storm's peak, the National Guard deployed across multiple communities, and mutual aid requests flooded in from over 90 cities and towns. As of Friday evening, fewer than 800 homes were still dark — a remarkable recovery, credit where it's due — but the damage to roads, infrastructure, and municipal budgets is just starting to come into focus.

Here's the part that doesn't make the feel-good press releases: storm response costs money that towns don't have sitting around. South Shore communities from Plymouth to Rockland were already staring at structural budget deficits heading into this week — Plymouth alone faces a $5.1 million spike in health insurance costs and had to cut eight firefighter positions just to present a balanced FY2027 budget. Now add blizzard cleanup on top. In New Bedford, Mayor Jon Mitchell thanked everyone from public works crews to Domino's Pizza for pitching in, and schools won't reopen until Monday. The storm was a stress test for municipal resilience, and the results should inform every budget conversation this spring.

What happens next: Town meetings are coming. Plymouth's Spring Town Meeting will grapple with a $343 million budget built on cuts and optimism. Across the South Shore, finance directors will be doing the math on storm costs and hoping the state comes through with some relief. Keep an eye on Beacon Hill.

What Else You Should Know
The Legislative Audit Fight Just Got Messier

State Auditor Diana DiZoglio and Attorney General Andrea Campbell are heading toward a full-blown constitutional showdown. DiZoglio filed an emergency motion with the Supreme Judicial Court this week after Campbell moved to throw out her lawsuit against legislative leaders — the same leaders who have stonewalled the audit that 72% of voters approved back in 2024. Campbell says DiZoglio can't file the suit without her office's blessing; DiZoglio says that's exactly the kind of gatekeeper problem voters were trying to fix. House Speaker Ron Mariano and Senate President Karen Spilka are still at the table, and nobody's blinking.

Rent Control Is Heading to the Ballot — and the Governor and Boston's Mayor Aren't on the Same Page

A proposed 2026 ballot question would cap annual rent increases at 5% statewide, and the political battle lines are already drawn: Mayor Michelle Wu says yes, Governor Healey says no. If that sounds confusing, it should — both are Democrats. The terminology alone (rent control vs. rent stabilization vs. rent caps) is enough to make your head spin, but the stakes are real: Massachusetts hasn't allowed rent control since voters banned it more than three decades ago, and housing costs have only gotten worse since.

Six Gateway Cities Get Downtown Development Fellows

Malden, Holyoke, Fitchburg, Chelsea, Peabody, and Lowell will each receive a full-time economic development fellow through MassDevelopment's Transformative Development Initiative — a three-year program aimed at filling vacant storefronts, supporting small businesses, and reviving commercial districts that have been hollowing out since the manufacturing era ended. Malden's mayor put it bluntly: with Orange Line access and commuter rail, vacant storefronts there make no sense, and he's hoping the program helps figure out why they keep sitting empty.

Pentagon Bans Military from MIT and Other "Woke" Universities

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced Friday that service members will be barred from attending Columbia, Yale, Brown, Princeton, MIT, and unnamed others starting next school year, calling them "factories of anti-American resentment." MIT is the obvious Massachusetts hit here — it's one of the most significant defense research institutions in the country, and the practical implications of severing those ties haven't been fully worked out. Hegseth offered no evidence for his claims and didn't specify exactly how broadly the ban applies.

Boston Lets Residents Decide $2.2 Million in City Spending — Here's What They Chose

More than 4,800 Bostonians voted in the city's second round of participatory budgeting, and they prioritized immigrant legal defense, access to fresh food, housing stability, workforce training, and youth financial literacy. It's a relatively small slice of a multi-billion-dollar budget, but the process itself — residents proposing, refining, and voting on real spending — is worth watching as a model for civic engagement.

The Shattuck Hospital Site Is Back on the Table

The Healey administration is reviving plans to redevelop the long-vacant 13-acre Lemuel Shattuck Hospital campus in Jamaica Plain, adjacent to Franklin Park — and community opposition is already organizing. Neighbors in JP, Roxbury, Dorchester, and Mattapan are mobilizing against any plan that includes supportive housing or addiction treatment, fearing spillover effects into the park. Nearly 100 residents showed up to the first listening session Thursday. This one is going to get loud.

One More Thing

Plymouth Town Manager Derek Brindisi had a line at this week's budget presentation that deserves to be cross-stitched onto a pillow in every selectman's office in Massachusetts: "Our expenses are outpacing our revenues... we haven't even got into the operating budget yet and we've already consumed over $7 million in new expenses." That's not a Plymouth problem. That's a Massachusetts-towns problem — and the blizzard just made it harder to look away.
