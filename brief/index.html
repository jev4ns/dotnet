The Audit War Heats Up — In Court and on Beacon Hill

State Auditor Diana DiZoglio is getting squeezed from every direction, and Wednesday was a rough one. A Supreme Judicial Court justice denied her bid to hire an outside attorney to represent her office in the lawsuit she's filed trying to force the Legislature to comply with voters' 2024 mandate to audit the House and Senate. Attorney General Andrea Campbell — who has declined to take DiZoglio's side — called the auditor's legal strategy a "ploy," and Justice Dalila Argaez Wendlandt agreed there was no legal basis for appointing outside counsel. DiZoglio now has to respond to Campbell's motion to strike the lawsuit entirely.

But even as that legal battle sputtered, DiZoglio showed up to a separate Beacon Hill hearing Wednesday to advocate for a ballot question that would extend the public records law to the Legislature and the governor's office — and the fireworks were real. Lawmakers who've been clashing with their former colleague for months came loaded for bear, while DiZoglio framed their resistance as proof of exactly why the law is needed. Massachusetts is already one of the weakest states in the nation on government transparency; it's the only state where the legislature is effectively exempt from public records law.

What happens next: DiZoglio's court case faces a critical deadline, and if the SJC dismisses it, the auditor will need a different strategy to enforce the will of voters. The public records ballot question, meanwhile, heads toward November — and given how her last ballot question did, don't count DiZoglio out yet.

ICE Raids Are Emptying Classrooms — and Draining School Budgets

The ripple effects of federal immigration enforcement are hitting Massachusetts school districts where it really hurts: the budget. Cities like Chelsea, where 46% of residents are foreign-born, have seen steep enrollment declines as families are deported or leave out of fear. Because state education funding is tied to enrollment numbers, fewer kids means fewer dollars — a compounding crisis that advocates want the Legislature to address now.

Boston City Council Unanimously Backs Wu's ICE Ban

After Councilor Ed Flynn pumped the brakes last month to do his "due diligence," the Boston City Council voted unanimously Wednesday to support Mayor Michelle Wu's executive order barring ICE from using city property for operations. The unanimous vote is a stronger signal than the original proposal would have sent, and it puts the full council on record backing the mayor as the city navigates its relationship with federal immigration authorities.

SJC Justices Sound Skeptical of Marshfield's Housing Law Complaint

The state's highest court didn't seem impressed Wednesday when Marshfield argued that the MBTA Communities Act — the 2021 law requiring communities near transit to allow multi-family housing by right — amounts to an unfunded mandate. Justices pressed the town's attorney on specifics: what exactly did this cost, and where in the law does it require the expensive modeling Marshfield claims it was forced to conduct? The attorney couldn't point to the language. With 222,000 new homes needed statewide by 2035, the stakes of this ruling go well beyond one South Shore town.

Vanna Howard Wins State Senate Seat in Lowell

State Rep. Vanna Howard has won the 1st Middlesex District Senate special election, pulling 4,276 votes compared to 1,693 for Republican Sam Meas and 1,370 for unenrolled candidate Joe Espinola. The seat was vacant following the death of Senator Edward Kennedy last year. Howard carried every community in the district except Dracut.

Massachusetts Tops the Nation on AP Exams — But Don't Pop the Champagne Yet

For the first time in state history, 35.8% of Massachusetts public high school graduates scored a 3 or higher on at least one AP exam — the best rate in the country. Participation is up too, with nearly half of graduates taking at least one test. Governor Maura Healey celebrated the milestone, but education leaders were quick to note the full picture: early literacy scores remain alarming, achievement gaps persist, and school districts from Boston to the Berkshires are wrestling with layoffs and shrinking enrollment as pandemic recovery money winds down.

Republicans Have a Ticket — Sort Of

The GOP race for governor got a little more structured this week. Republican gubernatorial candidate Mike Kennealy announced that Peabody City Councilor Anne Manning Martin will be his running mate. Manning Martin, a nearly two-decade council veteran who also works as deputy superintendent at a correctional hospital, has been critical of Governor Healey for focusing too much on fighting Trump and not enough on kitchen-table issues like energy costs. She joins Anne Brensley, who's been running for lieutenant governor on her own since September.

One More Thing

Marshfield's attorney showed up to the state's highest court to argue his town was burdened by "extraordinary" costs to comply with the MBTA housing law — and then couldn't tell the justices what those costs actually were when asked directly. Justice Dalila Wendlandt's question was simple: "What were the expenses?" Sometimes the most devastating legal moment is just a blank stare. Oral arguments, folks — they're not always pretty.
