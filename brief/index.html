The Lead
A Boston doctor faces 81 new charges. The scale of this case is staggering.

Dr. Derrick Todd, a 52-year-old Wayland rheumatologist, was back in a Boston courtroom Thursday to face 81 freshly returned indictments — including multiple counts of rape, indecent assault and battery, and assault with intent to rape — involving 22 alleged victims. He pleaded not guilty. This comes on top of charges already filed against him from an earlier round of indictments involving more than a dozen patients. The allegations span his time practicing in Massachusetts, and the sheer volume of charges suggests prosecutors have been building this case methodically for some time.

For anyone who trusted a physician with their care and their vulnerability, this case is a gut punch. The fact that Todd formerly practiced as a rheumatologist — treating patients with chronic pain conditions who often depend heavily on their doctors — makes the alleged betrayal all the more disturbing. The Massachusetts Board of Registration in Medicine has had its hands full lately, and the question worth asking now is: what did licensing authorities know, and when? Expect this case to intensify scrutiny on how the state monitors complaints against physicians.

Todd remains held without bail. The case heads back to Suffolk Superior Court, where prosecutors will now have to manage one of the largest patient-abuse prosecutions in recent Massachusetts memory.

🌿 Federal government reclassifies medical marijuana — and it matters here

The Trump administration officially moved state-licensed medical marijuana from Schedule I (alongside heroin) to Schedule III (alongside ketamine and some painkillers), a significant policy shift that legitimizes programs in Massachusetts and 39 other states. The change doesn't legalize marijuana federally, but it delivers a substantial tax break to licensed operators and opens the door to more robust medical research. A broader reclassification hearing is set for late June.

🗳️ Beacon Hill eyes making ballot initiatives harder

State lawmakers are increasingly frustrated with the citizen initiative process — and they're not hiding it. With a record 12 measures potentially headed to the 2026 ballot, some legislators want to tighten the rules, arguing that paid signature gatherers undermine the grassroots spirit the 1918 law intended. Critics of the reform push say it's Beacon Hill trying to protect its turf. Either way, the tension between direct democracy and legislative control is coming to a head.

⚡ AG Campbell to gas companies: overhaul your climate plans or face fines

Attorney General Andrea Campbell is putting Massachusetts' five major gas utilities — Eversource, National Grid, Unitil, Berkshire Gas, and Liberty — on notice. In new filings before the Department of Public Utilities, Campbell's office called the companies' climate compliance plans "completely inadequate" and urged regulators to impose financial penalties if the utilities don't submit substantially reworked strategies for transitioning away from fossil fuels. This is a pivotal moment for whether Massachusetts hits its ambitious emissions targets — and who ends up paying for the transition.

One More Thing

A South Shore teenager named Kaylee Joaquim, a student at South Shore Charter Public School, went on a college campus visit to UMass Dartmouth recently — and was bedridden for a week afterward from the exertion. She has long COVID, which now potentially affects nearly 6 million children across the country. "I'm never going on a field trip again," she said. "That's kind of sad." It is, Kaylee. It really is. Her story is a quiet reminder that for a lot of kids, the pandemic isn't over — it just got quieter.
