Healey's Cabinet Is Hemorrhaging — And the Timing Couldn't Be Worse

Seven of Governor Maura Healey's twelve Cabinet secretaries have walked out the door in the past year alone — an exodus that, by any objective measure, is unusual for a governor heading into a reelection cycle. As of this weekend, her administration's own website couldn't even be bothered to list current secretaries for education and housing. That's not a typo. The state's top officials for two of the most consequential policy areas in Massachusetts were effectively missing from the org chart.

Healey's allies have a ready explanation: the Trump administration is waging war on Massachusetts, slashing federal funding and steamrolling the state's priorities, and that pressure-cooker environment has made an already brutal job feel impossible. That's a real factor. But her Republican opponents aren't wrong to ask whether something more structural is going on inside the administration — whether the management culture, the workload, or the governor's own leadership style is accelerating departures that might otherwise be normal wear-and-tear. Former Governor Deval Patrick, no stranger to turbulent first terms, didn't lose Cabinet members at this pace.

What happens next: Healey needs to fill these seats, get her website in order, and — most importantly — offer the public a cleaner narrative before the reelection machinery kicks into full gear. Gaps at the top of government agencies don't just look bad; they slow down the work. And right now, Massachusetts has a lot of work to do.

The Legislature Still Doesn't Want to Be Audited

Voters passed a ballot question giving State Auditor Diana DiZoglio the authority to audit the Legislature. Lawmakers have been stonewalling ever since, declining to cooperate with DiZoglio's requests and sparring over the scope of what she can actually examine. The Boston Globe's political team digs into whether any version of this audit ever actually happens — and what it says about Beacon Hill's comfort with accountability when it applies to themselves.

Democrats Sue to Kill the All-Party Primary Ballot Question

Two veteran Democratic state committee members — Martina Jackson of Newton and Ann Roosevelt of Cambridge — have filed suit with the Supreme Judicial Court asking judges to toss a proposed ballot question that would replace partisan primaries with a California-style all-party system. The measure would likely pit two Democrats against each other in deep-blue Massachusetts general elections, which is exactly why party insiders are alarmed. Expect this to be a defining fight heading into November.

A Long-Blighted Somerville Block Is Finally Becoming Housing

The old Star Market on Broadway in Winter Hill — vacant and deteriorating for nearly two decades — has been demolished, and construction is underway on 319 new apartments across two six-story buildings, plus retail space and a public plaza. Somerville Mayor Jake Wilson, a Winter Hill resident himself, is thrilled. It's one of those "why did this take so long?" moments, but at least shovels are in the ground.

Fake Parking Tickets Are Circulating in Boston — Don't Scan That QR Code

Boston police are warning residents about convincing-looking fraudulent court notices that mimic official parking violation documents, complete with a judge's name, a Massachusetts state seal, a case number, and a QR code that leads to a scam payment site. Before you pay anything, verify through the city's official parking ticket portal. When in doubt, call 1-800-494-TIPS.

Marshfield's MBTA Zoning Fight Heads Toward a Legal Reckoning

The Marshfield Select Board voted 2-1 to put the MBTA Communities zoning bylaw back on the April Town Meeting warrant — just days before Town Counsel is set to argue the town's case against the state mandate before the Supreme Judicial Court. Vice Chair Steve Darcy warned the move could jeopardize millions in state grant funding. The South Shore is ground zero for the MBTA Communities battle, and Marshfield's court date will be worth watching closely.

Boston's Immigrant Services Office Wants More Money — A Lot More

Mayor Michelle Wu's Office of Immigrant Advancement is asking the City Council for a bigger slice of the FY2027 budget to fund legal defense for undocumented immigrants, as the federal immigration crackdown intensifies. Director Monique Tú Nguyen told councilors that 50 new calls for legal help arrive every week that the office can't immediately answer. The office's budget has already grown 21% over four years.

One More Thing

In Dracut, a 16-year-old named Mayra Gillis — whose twin brother is on the autism spectrum — personally delivered 10 sensory kits to local police and fire departments to help first responders communicate with kids in crisis. The kits include noise-reducing earmuffs, picture-based communication cards, and fidget tools. It's a small thing that could make an enormous difference in a scary moment for a child.
