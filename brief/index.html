The Lead
Beacon Hill Takes a Chainsaw to Mass Save
The Massachusetts House passed a sweeping energy bill Thursday night in a marathon session that laid bare just how much the affordability crisis has reshuffled Beacon Hill's priorities. The headline move: a $1 billion cut to Mass Save, the state's flagship energy efficiency program that helps residents and businesses electrify and weatherize their homes. The bill cleared 128–27, a lopsided margin that reflects both bipartisan anxiety about utility bills and real Democratic divisions about how far to pull back on the clean energy transition.
House budget chief Aaron Michlewitz framed the cut as putting money "back directly into the pockets of our constituents." And look, he's not wrong that people are furious about their utility bills. But critics — including the 27 dissenting votes — point out that slashing energy efficiency spending now could cost far more down the road, both in dollars and in the state's ability to hit its legally-binding climate targets. The bill also orders an Inspector General audit of Mass Save, a signal that lawmakers believe the program has real management problems worth scrutinizing before they hand it more ratepayer money.
The bill now heads to the Senate, where the debate starts all over again. Governor Maura Healey, who originally pushed for an "all of the above" energy approach, will need to decide how much of this she can live with. The clean energy coalition is already sharpening its knives for the Senate fight. Watch for Senate President Karen Spilka's chamber to soften the Mass Save cuts — but don't count on them going away entirely.

State Health Plan Drops Ozempic and Other GLP-1 Coverage
The Group Insurance Commission — which oversees health benefits for state employees, retirees, and their families — voted 10–7 Thursday to eliminate coverage of GLP-1 drugs like Ozempic and Wegovy for weight loss. Chair Valerie Sullivan called it a vote taken "with a heavy heart," citing a brutal budget year and fiduciary obligations. The seven dissenters warned the move could worsen chronic conditions and ultimately cost more. If you're a state worker who's been on one of these medications, start a conversation with your doctor now.

DiZoglio vs. The World (or At Least the AG and the Legislature)
The battle over auditing the Massachusetts Legislature keeps escalating. State Auditor Diana DiZoglio — backed by 72 percent of voters who approved the ballot measure in 2024 — filed an emergency motion this week after Attorney General Andrea Campbell moved to strike DiZoglio's lawsuit against legislative leaders. Campbell says she needs more information before she can go to court; DiZoglio says she's already provided it. Meanwhile, House Speaker Ron Mariano and Senate President Karen Spilka continue to stonewall. It's a three-way standoff with significant constitutional stakes.

MassDOT's Service Plaza Deal Called "Not a Model Procurement"
The Inspector General sent a formal investigatory letter to interim Transportation Secretary Phillip Eng, flagging serious problems with the Applegreen service plaza contract — the deal that helped push former Transportation Secretary Monica Tibbits-Nutt out the door. The IG found conflict of interest issues, contact rule violations by Applegreen, and evaluation committee members who didn't follow basic scoring protocols. The OIG stopped short of calling the whole deal invalid, but "not a model procurement" is the kind of phrase that tends to echo in court filings.

Rent Control Heads Toward a Voter Showdown
A proposed 2026 ballot question would cap annual rent increases at 5 percent statewide — and the political lines are already drawn. Boston Mayor Michelle Wu says yes. Governor Healey says no. GBH News has a useful breakdown of the terminology and tradeoffs for voters trying to cut through the noise before this lands on their ballots. The gap between "rent control" and "rent stabilization" matters more than you'd think.

South Shore Still Digging Out, But the End Is in Sight
Four days after the Blizzard of '26 buried the coast under two-plus feet of snow with 70 mph gusts, power restoration is nearly complete across the South Shore. Eversource said roughly 43,000 customers statewide should have lights back on by end of Friday, with Marshfield, Scituate, Plymouth, and Duxbury largely restored. In Hingham, Plymouth River Elementary School was evacuated Thursday after roof stress was detected near the cafeteria — students will spend Friday at Foster Elementary while crews clear the load. A good reminder that the storm's bill is still being paid.

Plymouth Faces a $9 Million Budget Hole — Sound Familiar?
Plymouth Town Manager Derek Brindisi brought some uncomfortable math to the Committee of Precinct Chairs this month: a projected $9 million FY2027 shortfall driven by a 14 percent spike in health insurance premiums and rising pension costs. The town patched the gap with $4.9 million in cuts, including eight firefighter positions not filled. With new development growth at Pine Hills and Redbrook beginning to plateau, Plymouth is learning what many towns are finding out — the revenue cavalry isn't coming.

One More Thing
Boston handed its residents $2.2 million and said: you decide. More than 4,800 people voted in the city's second "Ideas in Action" participatory budgeting cycle, and they chose to fund immigrant legal defense, fresh food access, housing stability, workforce training, and youth financial literacy. It's a small slice of a multi-billion-dollar city budget — but it's a genuinely good civics experiment, and the results suggest Boston residents are thinking about their neighbors.
