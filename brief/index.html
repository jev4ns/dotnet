The Lead
Beacon Hill Takes an Axe to Mass Save — and Sends It to the Senate

In a marathon session that ran well past 10 p.m. Thursday, the Massachusetts House passed a sweeping energy bill that cuts $1 billion from Mass Save — the state’s flagship energy efficiency program — and now heads to the Senate, where the real fight begins.

House budget chief Representative Aaron Michlewitz framed the cuts as relief for ratepayers hammered by sky-high utility bills, and that argument carried the day, 128–27. The bill targets Mass Save’s administrative, marketing, and advertising budgets — not its core efficiency work, supporters insist — and orders an inspector general review of the program. But critics on the left, including Representative Marjorie Decker, pushed back hard, arguing that pulling back on energy efficiency now is exactly the wrong move as the state races toward its climate commitments and renewable energy demand surges. Governor Maura Healey, who originally filed her own energy package last spring, has fashioned herself as an “all of the above” energy pragmatist. She’ll need all of that diplomatic flexibility as the Senate takes up the bill.

Here’s what to watch: the Senate has its own ideas about how to balance affordability with the clean energy transition, and Senate President Karen Spilka’s chamber has historically been friendlier to environmental priorities. Expect a conference committee fight. Whatever emerges will land on Healey’s desk with major implications for your utility bill and the state’s climate future.

What Else You Should Know
🌨️ Blizzard Recovery: Most Lights Are Back On, But It’s Not Over

Eversource says it has restored power to more than 426,000 customers since Monday’s historic nor’easter, with the final 43,000 or so expected back online by tonight — many of them on Cape Cod. Governor Maura Healey, who held a press briefing in Fall River on Thursday, called it a storm unlike anything the state has seen, comparing it to the Blizzard of ’78. Fall River Mayor Paul Coogan was refreshingly blunt: “We were not ready.” New Bedford schools stay closed through the weekend, and the South Shore coastal towns are still digging out.

🏫 Hingham School Evacuated After Roof Shows Signs of Stress

Plymouth River Elementary School in Hingham was cleared out Thursday after facilities staff spotted signs of structural stress near the cafeteria roof — the result of heavy snow accumulation from the blizzard. Students were bused to Hingham High School to finish the day, and Friday classes were relocated to Foster Elementary. A crane was brought in to help remove snow. It’s a vivid reminder that storm costs aren’t just in the cleanup — aging school buildings are quietly absorbing punishment all across the region.

🏛️ Mariano and Spilka Agree on Something: They Don’t Like Ballot Questions

House Speaker Ron Mariano and Senate President Karen Spilka — not always the best of friends — found common ground this week in their shared frustration with the ballot question process. With 11 potential questions headed for the fall ballot, both leaders complained that well-funded outside groups are essentially buying their way onto the ballot using paid signature-gathering firms. Mariano invoked California as a warning. Worth noting: two statewide officeholders, Secretary of State William Galvin and Auditor Diana DiZoglio, are themselves pursuing ballot questions — which, safe to say, is not helping the relationship with legislative leadership.

🎓 Senate Moves BRIGHT Act Forward for State College Upgrades

The Massachusetts Senate advanced the BRIGHT Act this week, a $3.28 billion bond bill that would modernize facilities at state colleges and universities — think 50-year-old science labs finally getting a refresh. The bill has backing from Northampton Senator Jo Comerford, who chairs the higher education committee, and would benefit UMass Amherst, Westfield State, Holyoke and Greenfield Community Colleges, Springfield Technical Community College, and more. The House has already passed its version. It’s not the flashiest legislation, but for students doing lab work in crumbling facilities, it’s a very big deal.

🏫 Six Gateway Cities Get Downtown Economic Development Help

Malden, Holyoke, Fitchburg, Chelsea, Peabody, and Lowell will each receive a full-time economic development fellow through MassDevelopment’s Transformative Development Initiative — a three-year commitment of on-the-ground expertise, grants, and technical assistance aimed at reviving downtown commercial districts. Malden Mayor Gary Christenson put the challenge bluntly: he can’t figure out why any storefront in a city with Orange Line access sits vacant. The program won’t solve everything, but having someone whose full-time job is answering that question is a start.

💰 Plymouth Facing a $9 Million Budget Hole

Town Manager Derek Brindisi delivered a sobering FY2027 budget preview to Plymouth officials this month: a $5.1 million spike in health insurance costs alone, representing a 14% increase in premiums, is eating through the town’s budget before anyone even gets to schools or public safety. Development-driven “new growth” from places like Pine Hills is plateauing, and the town had to cut eight firefighter positions from requests just to balance the books. Plymouth is a bellwether — what’s happening there is playing out in municipal budgets across the state.
