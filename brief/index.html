The House Passed a Major Energy Bill Last Night. Here's Why It's Complicated.

After a marathon session that pushed past 10 p.m. Thursday, the Massachusetts House passed a sweeping energy bill by a 128–27 vote — and the headline number is $1 billion in cuts to Mass Save, the state's flagship energy efficiency program. Before you cheer or jeer, though, the reality is messier than either side wants to admit.

Mass Save has a real problem: it costs a lot, and not all of that money goes toward actually weatherizing homes or electrifying heating systems. The House bill targets administrative overhead, marketing, and advertising budgets — not the rebates that help homeowners swap out oil burners for heat pumps. House budget chief Aaron Michlewitz framed it as a timeout, not a cancellation: get money back to struggling ratepayers now, audit the program, and rebuild smarter. That's a defensible position in a state where utility bills have become a genuine kitchen-table crisis. But clean energy advocates are furious, and not without reason — Massachusetts has legally binding climate targets, and Mass Save is one of the primary tools for hitting them. Pulling back now, even on overhead, sends a signal to the market and to momentum.

The bill now heads to the Senate, where the math and the politics will shift. Senate President Karen Spilka's chamber has generally been more protective of the clean energy framework. Expect the conference committee negotiations to be the real fight. Governor Healey, who has been threading an "all of the above" needle on energy, will ultimately have to decide what she can sign. 🌨️ Southeastern Mass. Is Still Digging Out — And It's Rough

Governor Healey was in Fall River Thursday, and her message was blunt: "We're not out of this yet." The historic nor'easter that dropped close to 40 inches in some South Shore and South Coast communities over 24 hours is still choking streets nearly a week later. Fall River Mayor Paul Coogan acknowledged his city wasn't ready for what turned out to be double the projected snowfall. About 9,800 customers remain without power, mostly on Cape Cod. Healey compared the storm to the Blizzard of 1978 — and that's not hyperbole.

🏫 A Hingham Elementary School Had to Evacuate Mid-Day Due to Storm Damage

Plymouth River Elementary School in Hingham was evacuated Thursday after the facilities team spotted signs of "structural stress" on the roof from heavy snow accumulation — 18 inches' worth. Students and staff were bused to Hingham High School to finish their day. Roofing specialists are working to clear the load, and kids will be at a different building again Friday. It's a reminder that the storm's ripple effects are far from over on the South Shore.

💊 State Workers Are Losing Coverage for Weight-Loss Drugs

The Group Insurance Commission voted Thursday to stop covering GLP-1 drugs like Ozempic and Mounjaro for weight loss under the health plan that covers state employees. About 22,000 members currently take these medications for weight loss, and the drugs have been a major driver of soaring premiums — up 10% two years running. Healey directed the GIC to find around $100 million in savings for the coming fiscal year, and this is one of the more painful places they found it. Nobody called it easy.

📊 Mariano and Spilka Agree on Something: They Don't Love Ballot Questions

House Speaker Ron Mariano and Senate President Karen Spilka — who don't always see eye to eye — found common ground Wednesday in complaining about the ballot initiative process. With 11 potential questions headed for the fall ballot, both leaders griped that well-funded interest groups have essentially industrialized signature gathering, turning a citizen tool into a lobbyist's shortcut. "The whole system is fraught with peril," Mariano said. This comes as Auditor Diana DiZoglio has filed suit to force a legislative audit — a measure 72% of voters approved in 2024 — and Secretary of State William Galvin is also pursuing his own ballot question. The Legislature's relationship with direct democracy is getting thornier by the week.

💻 Free Google AI Training for Every Massachusetts Resident

Healey announced Thursday that all Massachusetts residents will have free access to Google's suite of online professional training courses — normally $49/month — covering AI, cybersecurity, data analytics, and more. It's the third AI-related program she's announced in recent weeks, following a deal to bring ChatGPT to 40,000 state workers. Healey is clearly betting on AI as an economic development play heading into her reelection campaign, even as polls show many voters are skeptical of the technology.

🏘️ Plymouth Faces a $9 Million Budget Hole — And Healthcare Is the Culprit

Town Manager Derek Brindisi delivered a sobering FY2027 budget picture to Plymouth officials: a projected $9 million shortfall, driven largely by a 14% spike in health insurance premiums — a $5.1 million jump. The town patched the gap through cuts on both the municipal and school sides and by dipping into excess levy capacity, but that well won't last forever. With major development projects like Pine Hills and Redbrook maturing and delivering less new growth revenue, Plymouth is entering leaner budget years.
