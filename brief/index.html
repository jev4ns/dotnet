MassHealth Is Cutting $32 Million From Disability Services — And the People Affected Helped Design the Cuts

Here's a sentence you don't read very often: tens of thousands of Massachusetts seniors and people with disabilities are about to see reductions to a program that helps them get dressed, eat meals, and stay in their own homes — and some of the people who rely on that program helped decide where the cuts would fall. The state's Personal Care Attendant program, which serves about 56,000 residents, is trimming $32 million this summer, capping overtime hours for attendants and limiting how long they can spend on meal preparation. MassHealth assembled a working group of users, advocates, and providers throughout 2025 to guide the reductions — a model the agency says it plans to replicate as federal budget pressure forces more painful choices.

The honest read: this is genuinely a more humane approach than having bureaucrats make these calls behind closed doors. But it doesn't change the fact that people who depend on these services will have to scramble. Some will need to find additional help they may not be able to afford or locate. Assistant Secretary for MassHealth Mike Levine was unusually candid, saying there's "enormous urgency" driven by federal actions and that similar working groups are already forming for other long-term care programs. Expect this to be a recurring story all year as Washington's cuts ripple through Boston.

🏗️ Nearly Half a Billion for the Draw One Bridge

Governor Maura Healey announced $472 million in federal funding to replace the MBTA's North Station Draw One Bridge — the span that carries commuter rail over the Charles River between Boston and Cambridge. Total project cost is $1.2 billion, making it the single largest investment in MBTA history, with an estimated 15,600 jobs attached. The timing is notable: Healey is landing major federal infrastructure dollars even as the relationship between Massachusetts and the Trump administration stays frosty on other fronts.

🏫 ICE Activity Is Hitting School Budgets

Districts with large immigrant populations are getting squeezed in a way that doesn't show up in the immigration headlines: as families are deported or leave voluntarily, enrollment drops — and state aid follows enrollment. Chelsea lost roughly 350 students this year, translating to a $6 million budget hit. Holyoke lost 184. The Massachusetts Education Justice Alliance is pushing the Legislature to direct emergency funding to these districts before the damage becomes permanent.

🏘️ Marshfield Takes the MBTA Communities Act to the SJC

Marshfield's town attorney argued before the Supreme Judicial Court this week that the MBTA Communities Act — which requires towns near transit to zone for multi-family housing — is an unfunded mandate that coerces communities into compliance by threatening their state grants. The state's attorney general's office pushed back hard, saying the costs are minimal and the town's legal theory doesn't hold up. The justices have 130 days to rule, and the outcome could reshape how dozens of communities across the state handle zoning reform.

📋 DiZoglio Pivots to Ballot Strategy on Legislative Transparency

State Auditor Diana DiZoglio, after getting knocked down again in court in her bid to audit the Legislature, is now backing a ballot question that would expand public records law to cover the governor's office and the Legislature. She was blunt about her read of the situation at a State House hearing this week, calling the proceedings "a charade" and pointing to a letter from the Senate's legal team already opposing the idea. If lawmakers don't act by early May, the question heads to another round of signature gathering.

💸 Cohasset Schools Staring Down a $1.3 Million Hole

Cohasset's school district is facing a gut-wrenching $1.3 million shortfall for FY27, even after Superintendent Dr. Sarah Shannon trimmed a level-services budget request from nearly 12% growth down to 8.8%. The town offered a 3.62% increase. What's left is a list of teaching position eliminations, administrative restructuring, and the end of some athletic co-ops. Shannon called the funding structure a "structural shortfall" — and she's not wrong. This is the same math playing out in school districts from Hingham to Holyoke.

🚔 Plymouth County Sheriff Laying Off Crime Scene Unit — All of It

Sheriff Joe McDonald announced layoffs of 33 employees Thursday, including every single worker in the department's Bureau of Criminal Investigation — the unit that processes crime scenes for State Police and the FBI. McDonald cited an "unprecedented fiscal emergency" as state scrutiny of sheriff spending intensifies. This is one of the first concrete consequences of the Legislature's showdown with county sheriffs over chronic overspending, and it won't be the last.
