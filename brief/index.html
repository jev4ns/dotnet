The Lead
Wu's Streets Cabinet is grinding to a halt — and she did it to herself

Boston Mayor Michelle Wu built her political brand on buses, bikes, and safer streets. So it's striking — and more than a little ironic — that the Globe is reporting this morning that Wu quietly ordered a major policy change over a year ago requiring her personal sign-off on virtually every transit and street safety project in the city. The result: a near-total standstill on the kinds of infrastructure improvements that were supposed to define her tenure.

The backstory matters here. Wu implemented the new approval requirement after facing fierce political blowback over bike lanes — backlash so intense that her 2025 mayoral challenger made protected lanes a centerpiece of his campaign. But the cure appears to have been worse than the disease. According to eleven current and former Streets Cabinet employees who spoke with the Globe anonymously, the bottleneck has stalled the majority of city projects for more than a year, driven out top cabinet leaders, and left remaining staff demoralized. "These are going to be more promises that the city's breaking," one employee told the Globe.

What happens next is the real question. Wu is already in a politically awkward position — she skipped this morning's St. Patrick's Day Breakfast over her ongoing clash with South Boston's State Senator Nick Collins on property taxes, and the commuter rail derailment Friday reminded everyone the region's transit headaches are far from over. A mayor who ran as the city's transit champion can't afford to let her own Streets Cabinet become a monument to political caution. Expect this story to have legs.

What Else You Should Know

Immigrant enrollment collapse hits Massachusetts schools hard. Public school enrollment across the state dropped by more than 15,000 students between fall 2024 and fall 2025 — the lowest total in over three decades — and a sharp decline in English language learners appears to be a major driver. Gateway Cities including Brockton, Chelsea, Lawrence, and Lynn are seeing some of the steepest drops, with school leaders pointing directly to fear generated by intensified federal immigration enforcement. For districts that have relied on immigrant enrollment to offset declining birth rates, the financial and human consequences are real and growing. CommonWealth Beacon →

Vineyard Wind crosses the finish line — despite Trump. Offshore construction on Vineyard Wind was completed Friday night with the installation of the final turbine blades, making it the first major offshore wind farm to finish construction during the Trump administration — which had tried to stop it. Federal judges blocked the administration's halt order after finding no credible imminent national security threat, and the project pushed through. Meanwhile, a second project, Revolution Wind, began sending power to New England's grid on Friday. Score one for the courts, and for the region's clean energy future. WCVB →

Logan Airport workers sue over revoked security clearances. Four immigrant cabin cleaners who lost their jobs at Logan are now plaintiffs in a federal lawsuit accusing the Trump administration of using airport security rules as a pretext to push immigrants out of the workforce. U.S. Customs and Border Protection revoked the customs seals — credentials required to access secure airport areas — for at least 80 Logan workers after reinterpreting its own regulations last April. The Department of Homeland Security says its interpretation is sound. A federal judge in Boston will have to sort it out. Boston Globe →

South Shore schools face a brutal budget moment. In Rockland, the School Committee unanimously approved a $38.2 million FY2027 budget that eliminates 25 staff positions — 13 of them through direct layoffs — with Superintendent Alan Cron calling it the worst budget of his decade-long tenure. Health insurance costs alone surged 16%, or $1.7 million. Meanwhile, Duxbury voters at Saturday's Town Meeting approved a $1.89 million operating override and a feasibility study for the aging Alden Elementary School; the override now heads to a final town-wide ballot vote on March 28. A former BPS teacher gets 10 years for child exploitation. John Magee Gavin, 35, of Brookline — who taught science at the Josiah Quincy Upper School — was sentenced Friday in federal court after pleading guilty to coercing a minor online, receiving child pornography, and possession of child pornography. Investigators say Gavin used a Discord account to contact at least 20 underage girls across multiple states. He'll serve 10 years in federal prison followed by five years of supervised release. Southie threw a parade — Wu showed up, Collins cracked jokes. Hundreds of thousands turned out for the South Boston St. Patrick's Day Parade on Sunday, which ran in reverse this year (starting at Andrew Square) to honor the 250th anniversary of Evacuation Day. Police made 17 arrests. Transit Police confiscated a haul of borgs at Broadway station. And at the morning's St. Patrick's Day Breakfast, State Senator Nick Collins — who has blocked Wu's property tax legislation on Beacon Hill — got off the best line of the day about the mayor's absence: "We understand she found it just a little bit too taxing."
