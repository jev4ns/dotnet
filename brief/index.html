The Lead
The Person Who Helped Immigrant Communities Navigate the Storm Is Now Caught in It
Valentina Amaro Bowser has spent years as the human bridge between Governor Maura Healey's administration and Massachusetts' immigrant communities — answering calls from Spanish-language radio stations, nurturing relationships with Brazilian and Haitian newspapers, and helping families understand their rights amid a federal crackdown that has upended daily life across the state. Now she's on administrative leave, caught up in the same immigration enforcement machinery she was helping others survive.
Bowser, a Venezuelan native, is facing legal immigration troubles that the Healey administration won't detail publicly — but the timing and context are impossible to ignore. This is the governor's own multicultural media director, someone whose entire job was to make immigrant communities feel seen by state government, now sidelined by the very policies she was helping people navigate. Alberto Vasallo, president of El Mundo Boston, the region's oldest Spanish-language newspaper, put it plainly: "We're feeling her absence right now." These communities are already on edge — kids missing school, workers losing jobs — and losing their direct line to the governor's office is another wound on top of many.
What happens next depends on how the Healey administration handles both the legal situation and the communications vacuum Bowser's absence creates. It would be a mistake to treat this as simply an HR matter. For immigrant communities in Massachusetts right now, the difference between panic and calm can come down to a single trusted voice. That voice is gone for now.

White Stadium Price Tag Nearly Tripled — and City Hall Knew for Weeks
Mayor Michelle Wu knew Boston's share of the White Stadium rebuild had ballooned to $135 million — nearly three times the original $50 million estimate — at least a month before she told the public. A city building permit dated January 8 listed the project's declared value at $134 million, but Wu didn't announce the number until a February 6 press conference. The records came out only after a Herald public records request. This won't be the last we hear about it at the City Council.

The House Passed a Major Energy Bill — and Not Everyone's Happy
By a 128–27 vote, the Massachusetts House approved sweeping energy legislation that would cut roughly $1 billion from Mass Save's marketing and administrative budgets, expand clean energy procurement, ease restrictions on nuclear power, and return money to ratepayers. Supporters say it could save utility customers $9 billion over ten years. Environmental activists are furious, arguing the Mass Save cuts would gut a program that's been central to the state's climate strategy. The bill now heads to the Senate, where the real fight begins.

Federal Cuts Could Gut Housing Programs for 4,000 Massachusetts Households
The Trump administration wants to roll back "Housing First" — the two-decade-old approach that prioritizes getting people into stable housing before addressing mental health or addiction issues. In Massachusetts, that shift could jeopardize $91 million in federal funding supporting nearly 4,000 households. Some programs are already declining new clients. In Northampton, a facility serving 14 medically complex residents — people who've lost limbs, who are paralyzed, who live with severe mental illness — may have to scale back or close. These are real people who were homeless and aren't anymore. That's what's at stake.

State Police Face Contempt Hearing Over Withheld Dash Cam Footage
A Middlesex County Superior Court judge hears arguments Tuesday on whether the Massachusetts State Police should be fined for allegedly withholding dash cam footage from a 2023 cruiser crash involving Sergeant Scott Quigley, who may have been drunk at the time. The footage — which shows officers debating whether to delay interviewing Quigley — never made it to defense attorneys in an ongoing first-degree murder trial involving three brothers whose cases Quigley was working before the crash. The State Police claim they'd performed a "diligent search." The footage then appeared in the Herald. Not a great look.

TSA Workers at Logan Are Working Without Pay — Again
A partial government shutdown means TSA agents at Logan Airport and across the country have stopped receiving paychecks, the second time in recent months this has happened. The union representing New England TSA workers says morale is suffering and agents will eventually face hard choices about whether they can keep showing up to work for free. That's not just a labor story — it's a travel story. Watch Logan wait times.

A Major Investor Is Pumping the Brakes on Boston Real Estate
Jeff Kanne, CEO of National Real Estate Advisors, which manages about $10 billion in pension money, says Mayor Wu's policies and the prospect of statewide rent control on the November ballot are making other cities more attractive than Boston. He's not a speculator — his clients are electricians' retirement funds. When that kind of cautious, long-horizon money starts looking elsewhere, it's worth paying attention to what's driving the hesitation.
