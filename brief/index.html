The Lead
Holden Draws a Line in the Sand — and Faces the Legal Consequences

For the second time in less than a year, Holden voters have told the state to take its housing law and shove it. Monday night's special town meeting rejected a proposal to comply with the MBTA Communities Act by a crushing 520–257 margin — more than two to one — leaving town officials with no remaining cards to play and Attorney General Andrea Campbell's lawsuit already in motion.

This isn't a close call or a procedural misfire. Town Manager Peter Lukes put it plainly: residents were fully informed, they debated it for months, and they still said no. Twice. What happens next is fairly predictable if you watched the Milton saga play out: Campbell's lawsuit will likely move through the courts, and Holden will almost certainly lose. The SJC already ruled in January 2025 that the MBTA Communities Act is constitutional and mandatory. The only question is how long Holden wants to pay legal fees to delay the inevitable — and what state funding it forfeits in the meantime.

Meanwhile, over on the South Shore, Marshfield is heading in the opposite direction — sort of. The town, which actually passed its MBTA zoning bylaw last October, has now scheduled an April 27 town meeting to reconsider that vote. So we've got one town fighting to get out of compliance and another potentially fighting to get back out. The MBTA Communities Act saga is far from over in the suburbs.

🏫 Dyslexia Screening Has No Teeth — Yet

Massachusetts mandated twice-yearly dyslexia screening for K-3 students back in 2023, but there's a glaring hole: nobody actually checks whether schools are doing it. The state Senate included a reporting requirement in its sweeping literacy bill; the House stripped it out. Advocates say it's a small fix with enormous consequences for early identification — and now it's headed to a conference committee where its fate is uncertain.

🏗️ Healey Wants to Overhaul How Big Projects Get Built

Buried in the governor's budget proposal is a measure that could fundamentally change how Massachusetts handles its most complex infrastructure jobs — the Cape Cod bridges, the Allston Pike realignment, the Storrow Drive tunnel. The "progressive design-build" approach would bring in private partners early, before designs are finalized, rather than handing out contracts to the lowest bidder after the fact. Supporters say it produces better outcomes faster. Critics will argue it reduces competition and accountability. Either way, MBTA chief Phil Eng is clearly done pretending the old model is working.

⚖️ DiZoglio's Legislature Audit Takes Another Hit

State Auditor Diana DiZoglio's campaign to audit the Legislature suffered a significant blow Tuesday when a single SJC justice blocked her from hiring outside counsel — specifically Shannon Liss-Riordan, who ran against AG Andrea Campbell in 2022. The judge said DiZoglio's team cited no legal authority that would allow the court to appoint a special attorney. Campbell has already asked the high court to kill the lawsuit entirely, and DiZoglio now has until Thursday to respond. The walls are closing in on this one.

🏘️ A Ballot Push to "Legalize Starter Homes"

A Pioneer Institute-backed ballot committee wants Massachusetts voters to approve a statewide zoning change that would allow small single-family homes — think modest, starter-level housing — on any residentially zoned lot of at least 5,000 square feet with access to public sewer and water. The idea, modeled on a Maine law passed last year, wouldn't upend local control entirely, but it would override the oversized minimum-lot requirements that have quietly driven up housing costs and squeezed out first-time buyers for decades.

📚 Massachusetts Students Hit No. 1 on AP Exams

Good news worth savoring: Massachusetts public high school graduates led the nation in Advanced Placement exam performance last year, with 35.8 percent scoring a 3 or higher — a new all-time state record. The state also ranked near the top for Black and African American student participation in AP courses. Credit where it's due: expanded exam fee coverage and early college programs appear to be paying off.

💰 Quincy Councilors Stuck With Raises They Hate

Seven newly elected Quincy City Council members — swept in last fall on a wave of outrage over a pay raise the outgoing council approved without a single public hearing — are now legally required to collect that same $15,500 raise themselves. State law prevents them from immediately repealing it. Several plan to donate the extra money. The proposed 79% raise for Mayor Thomas Koch, originally set at $285,000, has been trimmed and pushed to 2028 — cold comfort for residents still fuming.
