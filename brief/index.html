The Lead

A Healey Aide's Story Puts a Human Face on the Immigration Crisis

Valentina Amaro Bowser has spent years being the voice of Governor Maura Healey's administration to Massachusetts' immigrant communities — answering questions in Spanish on local radio, fielding calls from Brazilian and Haitian newspapers, helping terrified families navigate the Trump administration's crackdown. Now she's caught inside it. Bowser, a Venezuelan native who has lived here legally since 2013, was placed on leave after her work visa situation became entangled in the Trump administration's blanket pause on immigration applications from dozens of countries, including Venezuela.

Late Friday, a federal judge gave the Healey administration a partial win: US District Judge Angel Kelley ordered federal officials to act within 10 days on Bowser's work authorization application, ruling the couple was "likely to succeed" on their claim that the hold is "arbitrary and capricious." That's a meaningful legal rebuke — but it doesn't resolve the underlying problem, and Bowser remains sidelined while the communities she served feel the gap. Alberto Vasallo, president of El Mundo Boston, put it plainly: "We're feeling her absence right now."

What happens next depends on whether the Trump administration complies with Kelley's order or drags its feet — and on how the courts continue to handle the cascade of immigration challenges landing on their dockets. Bowser's case has become something larger than one person's work visa: it's a concrete illustration of how broadly the administration's policies are reaching, touching even state government employees who built careers helping others navigate this exact system.

Massachusetts Reacts to U.S.-Israel Strikes on Iran — and a War Nobody Voted For

The joint U.S.-Israeli military operation that killed Iranian Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei is a world-historical event with real Massachusetts angles: hundreds of Iranian Americans gathered in Copley Square to celebrate what they hope is the end of the regime, while protesters on Boston Common condemned the strikes as illegal. Congressman Stephen Lynch was direct in his alarm, saying Trump never consulted Congress and warning that American troops could be put "in harm's way without a really good strategy." Strikes are reportedly continuing this week, and the situation is fluid at best.

The South Shore's Budget Squeeze Is a Structural Problem, Not Just a Tight Year

Communities from Plymouth to Rockland are staring at a math problem that creative accounting can't fix much longer: expenses outrunning revenues, state aid falling short, and a special education transportation system that's structurally broken. A new state Inspector General report found that the circuit breaker reimbursed transportation costs at only 61% last year instead of the statutory 75% — and at $13,825 per rider, that gap hits municipal budgets hard. The recent blizzard only underscored the point: resilience costs money too.

Massachusetts Has Five Years to Prepare for an Elder Care Cliff — and It's Not Ready

The oldest baby boomers turn 85 in 2031, and 85 is when the need for intensive care — home health aides, assisted living, memory care — spikes dramatically. A new analysis in CommonWealth Beacon lays out the scale of what's coming: the 85-and-over population in Massachusetts will grow by roughly two-thirds in the coming decades, and we're already short on workers and capacity. This is the kind of slow-moving crisis that gets ignored until it's a full emergency.

Clean Energy Gets New Leadership

The Massachusetts Clean Energy Center board voted to appoint Ben Downing as its next CEO. Downing brings an unusual mix of credentials: a decade in the State Senate where he helped authorize the state's first offshore wind procurement, followed by stints at MIT's climatetech accelerator The Engine and renewable energy company Nexamp. MassCEC oversees billions in clean energy investment, and the pick signals continuity with the Healey administration's aggressive climate agenda — which is increasingly swimming against federal currents.

Boston Councilor Takes Aim at Youth Participatory Budget

City Councilor Ed Flynn wants to slash Boston's participatory budgeting program — which lets residents as young as 11 vote on how $2 million in city funds gets spent — down to $100,000 or $200,000. Flynn framed it as fiscal discipline amid Mayor Michelle Wu's directive that all departments trim FY27 budgets by 2%, driven by declining commercial property values. Whether you think giving kids a stake in civic decisions is visionary or naive, the fight previews what's going to be a brutal Boston budget season.

SNAP Fraud Letter Complicates Healey's "Distraction" Argument

A 2024 letter from the USDA — sent by the Biden administration — urged Governor Healey to clean up Massachusetts' SNAP program, which failed to meet federal standards on payment error rates and application processing. Healey has called Trump's focus on Massachusetts fraud a political "distraction," but the letter makes that argument harder to sustain. The state's food assistance system has legitimate oversight problems that predate the current political fight.
