The Lead
The Blizzard That Won't Let Go

Three days after one of the worst nor'easters in living memory, Southeastern Massachusetts is still digging out — and the human cost is becoming clearer by the hour. Governor Maura Healey stood in Fall River Thursday alongside Mayor Paul Coogan, who didn't mince words: "We were not ready. We anticipated 18 inches, and we got double that." Nearly 30,000 customers remained without power statewide by Thursday evening, with Cape Cod bearing the brunt. Four emergency shelters remain open on the Cape, housing roughly 200 people — most of them elderly with medical needs.

The storm is exposing some uncomfortable truths. On the Cape, where more than 40% of the population is over 60, a traveling nurse named Colleen Wright spent days pushing through downed trees and unplowed roads to check on homebound patients. In Hingham, Plymouth River Elementary had to be evacuated mid-school day after the roof showed "signs of structural stress" from snow load. A Kingston building wasn't so lucky — an abandoned athletic facility collapsed entirely. Meanwhile, in Boston, Mayor Michelle Wu extended the parking ban by nearly an extra day to allow more thorough snow removal, a tweak she credited for a noticeably faster cleanup compared to January's storm.

What happens next: Healey has already signaled this storm will likely qualify for federal disaster assistance — that process takes weeks. The Cape's power restoration is the immediate priority, but the longer story is about infrastructure. From rooftop collapses to overwhelmed municipal plowing operations, the storm is making a blunt argument that communities from Fall River to Barnstable need to invest more in climate resilience. The question is whether the Legislature is listening.

What Else You Should Know
🏛️ Beacon Hill's leaders take aim at the ballot box — kind of

House Speaker Ron Mariano and Senate President Karen Spilka don't agree on much, but they found common ground this week in complaining about the ballot question process. With 11 potential questions headed for review, both leaders aired frustration that well-funded outside groups are essentially buying their way onto the ballot through paid signature-gathering firms. Mariano invoked the specter of California. Whether their griping leads to actual reform of the process — or just venting — remains to be seen.

💉 Ozempic era over for state workers

The Group Insurance Commission voted Thursday to stop covering GLP-1 drugs like Zepbound and Mounjaro for weight loss for the roughly 22,000 state employees and retirees who currently use them. The move is part of a directive from Governor Healey to find $100 million in savings after two straight years of 10% premium increases. Nobody on the board called it a good decision — just a necessary one. Expect this to be a flashpoint in upcoming union negotiations.

🚂 The T is staring down a 'fleet cliff'

The MBTA board got some sobering news Thursday: half of the locomotives hauling 100,000-plus commuter rail riders on a typical weekday are nearing the end of their useful life. The T is moving to buy 20 new locomotives — 10 battery-electric, 10 diesel — with a contract award expected this summer. The electric units are slated for the Providence Line, the only commuter rail corridor already wired for electrified service. If the T doesn't act fast, agency officials warn a locomotive shortage could hit by the early 2030s.

🏫 Special ed transportation is broken, and the IG wants action

A new report from Inspector General Jeffrey Shapiro confirms what many school business managers have known for years: Massachusetts' system for reimbursing school districts for special education transportation costs is a mess. Vendors aren't required to provide itemized invoices, there's no central database of contracts, and the state's "circuit breaker" reimbursement model leaves districts holding the bag. The IG is calling for "meaningful action." Now it's on the Legislature and DESE to respond.

🏙️ Somerville's new mayor wants to build his way out of a housing crisis

Newly elected Mayor Jake Wilson bought his first Somerville home 21 years ago for $425,000 — a price that felt outrageous at the time and now sounds like a fantasy. His plan to address the city's housing crunch includes more density, new zoning, and fresh investment in affordable and social housing. The challenge: Somerville is already New England's most densely populated city, and the politics of adding density are never simple, even in a progressive enclave.

🤖 Free Google AI courses for every Massachusetts resident

Healey announced Thursday that all Massachusetts residents will get free access to Google's professional certificate courses — normally $49 a month — covering AI, cybersecurity, data analytics, and more. It's her third AI-focused announcement in recent weeks, following a ChatGPT deal for 40,000 state workers and a $100 million AI investment fund. The political tightrope: enthusiasm for AI is not exactly universal, and some in her own party think the governor is moving too fast.
