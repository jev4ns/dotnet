The Lead
The Blizzard Is Over. The Reckoning Is Just Beginning.

Governor Maura Healey lifted the state of emergency Friday night, officially closing the books on what is already being called the Blizzard of '26 — a nor'easter that buried southeastern Massachusetts under several feet of snow, knocked out power to more than 291,000 customers, and sent mutual aid requests flooding in from over 90 communities. By Friday evening, fewer than 800 residents remained in the dark, down from that staggering peak. The National Guard assisted with snow removal in Duxbury and Brockton, and more than a dozen state agencies were deployed. In New Bedford, Mayor Jon Mitchell described reaching "an inflection point" — roads mostly passable, attention now shifting to excavation. Schools there reopen Monday.

But the real story isn't the storm itself — it's what the storm exposed. The South Shore's fiscal situation was already precarious before Monday. Plymouth's town manager was already warning of a $9 million budget shortfall driven by a 14% spike in health insurance premiums. Rockland, Whitman, and others are staring at structural deficits that can't be papered over much longer. Then the blizzard arrived and stress-tested every municipal emergency operation on the coast simultaneously. Resilience, it turns out, isn't free — and the towns that got hit hardest are also the ones with the least margin to absorb surprise costs.

The cleanup will wind down. The budget math won't. Watch for town meetings across the South Shore this spring to be unusually tense.

The Legislature Audit Fight Gets Messier

State Auditor Diana DiZoglio filed an emergency motion with the Supreme Judicial Court this week after Attorney General Andrea Campbell moved to strike DiZoglio's lawsuit against legislative leaders — the one trying to force House Speaker Ron Mariano and Senate President Karen Spilka to comply with an audit that 72% of voters approved back in 2024. Campbell says DiZoglio doesn't have standing to file the suit without her office's sign-off. DiZoglio says Campbell is running interference for the Legislature. Voters approved this. Someone should probably explain that to Beacon Hill.

Rent Control Heads Toward a Ballot Showdown

Massachusetts voters may get to decide in November whether to cap annual rent increases at 5% statewide — and the political lines are already drawn. Mayor Michelle Wu is for it. Governor Healey is against it. The terminology alone — "rent control" vs. "rent stabilization" — is a battleground. Harvard urban planning lecturer Alexander von Hoffman broke down what the policy would actually do, and it's worth understanding before the campaign spin machine kicks into overdrive.

Plymouth's Budget Crunch Is a Preview for Towns Everywhere

Town Manager Derek Brindisi's FY2027 budget presentation was not a fun meeting: a $5.1 million health insurance spike, plateauing development revenue from Pine Hills and Redbrook, and cuts that included eight firefighter positions. The town started with a $9 million hole and clawed back to balance through $4.9 million in combined cuts. This is the story playing out in dozens of Massachusetts municipalities right now — Plymouth is just further along in telling it honestly.

Pentagon Bans Military Attendance at MIT and Other Universities

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced Friday that service members will be barred from attending MIT, Columbia, Yale, Brown, Princeton, and others starting next school year, calling them "factories of anti-American resentment." No evidence was cited. MIT remains listed as eligible on official Pentagon websites, and the scope of the ban remains murky. For Massachusetts, where MIT is a major hub of federally funded research and ROTC activity, this is worth watching closely.

Boston Residents Voted on $2.2M in City Spending — Here's What Won

More than 4,800 Bostonians participated in Mayor Wu's second cycle of participatory budgeting, and the results reflect the city's priorities: immigrant legal defense, fresh food access, housing stability, workforce training, and youth financial literacy all made the cut. It's a small slice of Boston's overall budget, but the process itself — letting residents actually decide — is worth paying attention to as a model.
