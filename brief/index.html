The Lead

Healey Lifts Emergency — But the South Shore's Recovery Is Far From Over

Governor Maura Healey formally ended the state of emergency Friday night, four days after the Blizzard of '26 buried Southeastern Massachusetts under several feet of snow and knocked out power to more than 291,000 customers at its peak. By Friday evening, fewer than 800 residents remained in the dark — a remarkable turnaround that required over 3,000 pieces of MassDOT equipment, 200 MEMA assistance requests, National Guard deployments, and mutual aid from four other states and Canada. That's not routine storm response. That's a small army.

The hard numbers tell you how historic this was. South Shore coastal towns and Cape Cod bore the brunt of it — Marshfield, Scituate, Plymouth, and Duxbury were among the last to get the lights back on, with Humarock practically cut off for days. Cities like New Bedford and Brockton relied on the National Guard just to dig out their streets. Mayor Jon Mitchell of New Bedford described Friday as "an inflection point," with crews pivoting from making roads passable to the harder, slower work of actual snow excavation. Schools there reopen Monday.

What comes next isn't just cleanup — it's the bill. As South Shore News documented this week, communities across the region were already staring at structural budget problems before a single snowflake fell. The storm was, as that outlet put it, "an unplanned stress test for municipal emergency operations." The costs of resilience — mutual aid, emergency equipment, overtime — layer on top of budgets that were already cracking. Expect this to show up in town meeting season in a very uncomfortable way.

What Else You Should Know

The Audit Saga Gets Messier — and Heads to the SJC

State Auditor Diana DiZoglio filed an emergency motion with the Supreme Judicial Court this week after Attorney General Andrea Campbell moved to throw out DiZoglio's lawsuit against legislative leaders entirely. Campbell says DiZoglio doesn't have the authority to file suit without her office's approval. DiZoglio says Campbell is running interference for the Legislature. Meanwhile, 72% of voters approved giving the auditor exactly this power back in 2024 — and the Legislature has stonewalled ever since. House Speaker Ron Mariano and Senate President Karen Spilka aren't budging, citing separation of powers concerns. This one's headed for a landmark ruling.

Rent Control Standoff: Wu Says Yes, Healey Says No, Voters May Decide

Massachusetts hasn't had rent control since voters banned it in 1994, but a 2026 ballot question could cap annual rent increases at 5% statewide and reopen one of the most contentious debates in housing policy. Boston Mayor Michelle Wu is firmly in favor; Governor Maura Healey is firmly opposed. With the terminology — rent control vs. rent stabilization vs. rent caps — deliberately slippery on all sides, GBH News broke down what the ballot question would actually do and what the research says. Worth reading before the campaign noise gets louder.

Six Gateway Cities Get Economic Development Help Downtown

Malden, Holyoke, Fitchburg, Chelsea, Peabody, and Lowell were selected this week for MassDevelopment's Transformative Development Initiative, each receiving a full-time economic development fellow for three years, plus grants and technical assistance to help revive blighted downtowns. Malden Mayor Gary Christenson told CommonWealth Beacon he's baffled by vacant storefronts in a city with Orange Line and commuter rail access — and he's hoping the program finally figures out why. Given the budget pressures hammering municipalities right now, the timing is, as one official put it, something that "couldn't come at a better time."

Plymouth Staring Down a $9 Million Budget Hole

Before the blizzard hit, Plymouth Town Manager Derek Brindisi was already delivering a sobering message: a 14% spike in health insurance premiums — a $5.1 million jump — combined with plateauing new-growth revenue has the town dipping into reserves and cutting department requests just to present a balanced $343 million FY2027 budget. Eight firefighter positions were among the cuts on the table. With Spring Town Meeting approaching, Plymouth's fiscal stress is a preview of conversations happening in town halls across the South Shore right now.

Pentagon Bans Military Attendance at MIT and Other Universities

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced Friday that military personnel will be barred from attending MIT, Columbia, Yale, Brown, Princeton, and "many others" starting next school year, calling them "factories of anti-American resentment." MIT's inclusion is significant for Massachusetts — the institute has deep, longstanding ties to Defense Department research and military programs. The exact scope of the ban remains unclear, and as of Friday the affected schools were still listed as eligible on Pentagon websites.

Boston Residents Voted on $2.2 Million in City Spending — Here's What Won

More than 4,800 Bostonians voted — online and in person — on how to spend $2.2 million in city funds through Mayor Michelle Wu's participatory budgeting program. The winning projects include immigrant legal defense funding, expanded access to healthy food, housing stability programs, workforce training, and youth financial literacy initiatives. Eight projects total will begin receiving funding this spring. Love it or find it gimmicky, it's a real-money experiment in direct democracy at the neighborhood level.
